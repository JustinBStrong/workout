Workout

This is a seven-session cycle, not a seven-day calendar week. Perform the sessions in order and insert rest days whenever recovery requires them. When the knee is sensitive, use the temporary version of Session 1 rather than deleting that session and compressing all of the upper-body workouts together.

Session details

Choose one option where alternatives are listed and keep it for a block of approximately 6–8 cycles. Do not select a different variation every time merely for novelty.

Session 1 — Knee-Light Legs + QL

  1. Band-above-knee squat preparation — 2 light sets
  2. Dead bug — 2 controlled sets
  3. Belt squat / hack squat / leg press / goblet squat — choose one, 3 × 8–12
  4. Reverse lunge / low step-up — choose one, 2 × 8–12 each side
  5. Seated calf raise — 4 × 8–15
  6. Tibialis raise — 3 × 15–25
  7. Hip abductor machine or cable abduction — 3 × 15–25
  8. Hip adductor machine — 3 × 15–25
  9. Pallof press — 2–3 × 10–12 each side
  10. Side plank — 2–3 × 20–40 seconds each side

Why this order: The banded preparation rehearses knee tracking without meaningful fatigue, while the dead bug establishes trunk control before loaded lower-body work. There is only one main knee-dominant exercise and one modest unilateral exercise. Calves, tibialis, and hip work add lower-body volume without repeating another squat pattern. Pallof presses and side planks train anti-rotation and anti-lateral flexion after the leg work so the QL is not pre-fatigued before the loaded movements.

Serious lifting movements: 6
Dedicated rehab/control exposures: 4

Current knee-sensitive version: Omit movements 3 and 4 if they reproduce the sensitivity. Keep the calves, tibialis, hip work, Pallof press, and side plank so this remains a real session and recovery spacer rather than being deleted from the cycle. Use the banded preparation only if it also feels comfortable. Because the location and cause of the knee sensitivity have not been diagnosed, this is load reduction rather than treatment for a presumed condition. Squatting, stairs, and running can all aggravate several different anterior-knee presentations, so treadmill and 4×4 work should also be judged by their symptom response.

  6. Bayesian cable curl — 2 × 10–15

Why this order: The wall slide rehearses upward rotation and posterior tilt before vertical pulling. The main pull is performed fresh. The unilateral pulldown adds side-to-side control and a different line of shoulder extension. The straight-arm pulldown trains loaded downward rotation and lat capacity without adding another elbow-flexion-heavy pull. The high cable lat row remains elbow-to-hip and lat-oriented rather than duplicating the horizontal thickness day. Biceps receive only a small direct exposure. The prone Y restores lower-trap and upward-rotation control after the lats have been heavily used.

Scapular job: upwardly rotated reach at the top, followed by controlled downward rotation and depression.

Face-pulls finish with retraction, posterior tilt, and external rotation.

After Session 7, return to Session 1. That places the knee-light lower session between the heavy arm day and the next back-width session, preventing the biceps from being trained hard on consecutive sessions.

Progression and effort

Primary compounds: Use a top set at approximately RPE 8–9, followed by back-off work around RPE 7–8. Add load when all back-off sets reach the top of the prescribed range with stable technique.

Bench: When the top set reaches 5 clean repetitions and the three back-off sets reach 8, add 2.5–5 pounds. Microplates are reasonable as the bar approaches 225. The close-grip paused bench should remain subordinate to the regular bench rather than becoming a second maximal lift.

Accessories: Most sets should finish with approximately 0–2 clean repetitions in reserve. The final set of stable curls, pushdowns, lateral raises, calf raises, and machines may approach failure when technique remains intact.

Rehabilitation and control work: Keep approximately 2–4 clean repetitions in reserve. Do not grind, chase load, or continue once the scapular movement becomes less controlled. Failure is not required for strength or hypertrophy, and avoiding it is particularly sensible where the purpose is motor control.

QL work: Progress suitcase carries through cleaner posture, distance, and then load. Progress side planks through longer controlled holds before adding weight. Neither should create the familiar grabbing or spasm pattern.

Knee work: Reintroduce only one loaded knee pattern first. Restore the unilateral movement after the first pattern is tolerated across multiple exposures. Swelling, locking, giving way, progressively worsening pain, or pain that does not settle with reduced loading warrants assessment rather than continued experimentation.

Your treadmill and 4×4 work remain outside the written program, but they still contribute to quadriceps, calf, and knee loading. During the current sensitive period, their dose should be adjusted by the same symptom response used for the knee-dominant lifting.

Do not hold the shoulder blades “back and down” during every exercise. When reaching, the scapula should wrap forward around the ribs. During arm elevation, it should upwardly rotate and posteriorly tilt while generally moving toward external rotation. During rows it should glide backward and then forward again on the return. During carries and heavy holds it should remain broad and controlled rather than maximally pinched together. Fatigue can reduce posterior tilt and increase internal rotation, which is one reason control work should not be ground into failure.

The inferior angle is the bottom point of the shoulder blade. The medial border is the inner edge nearest the spine. The acromion is the bony roof at the top and outside of the shoulder. The most useful general serratus cue is:

Make the shoulder blade wrap around the ribs while reaching long.

External rotation

Glenohumeral external rotation means that the upper-arm bone rotates outward inside the shoulder socket. With the elbow beside the torso, the forearm moves away from the stomach. In a 90/90 position, the forearm rotates backward while the elbow remains approximately fixed.

Rotate the upper arm rather than merely turning the hand or wrist. Keep the front of the shoulder from rolling forward, maintain a stacked rib cage, and allow modest scapular retraction and posterior tilt without squeezing the shoulder blades together as hard as possible.

The useful general cue is:

Turn the inside of the elbow forward while the shoulder remains centered.

  1. Face-pull to external rotation. Pull first, allowing the scapulae to retract without forcing them downward. Then rotate the upper arms outward. Keep the wrists quiet, the fronts of the shoulders centered, and the scapulae gently posteriorly tilted rather than aggressively pinched.

  2. Prone ER 90/90. Keep the elbow approximately fixed while the upper arm rotates in the socket. Maintain a gently stable, posteriorly tilted scapula and stop before the front of the shoulder rolls forward or the torso arches.

  3. Side-lying external rotation. Keep the elbow against the side and rotate the upper arm without rolling the torso backward. The scapula should remain quietly supported against the ribs rather than being forcefully retracted. Stop when further range would require the shoulder to tip forward or the torso to rotate.

  4. Prone W. Rotate the upper arms outward as the elbows travel slightly backward. Keep the neck long and think of the upper scapula tipping backward rather than dragging the entire blade toward the back pocket.

  5. Arnold press. The dumbbell and upper-arm rotation is glenohumeral rotation, while the scapula simultaneously upwardly rotates, externally rotates, and posteriorly tilts. The full scapular sequence appears under Arnold press.

Common errors are moving the hand without truly rotating the upper arm, allowing the elbow to drift backward to create false range, arching the torso, violently retracting the scapulae, and continuing after the front of the shoulder loses its centered position.

Horizontal abduction

Horizontal abduction means that the upper arm moves outward and backward while raised away from the side, as in a reverse fly. It is an upper-arm movement that normally occurs alongside scapular retraction, but they are not identical.

Move the upper arms wide and backward while allowing the scapulae to glide together naturally. Keep the fronts of the shoulders from rolling forward and stop before additional range comes from thrusting the chest or arching the spine.

The useful cue is:

Arms wide; let the blades glide together instead of crushing them together.

  1. Reverse pec deck or rear-delt cable fly. Move through the upper arms while the scapulae retract naturally. Stop when the arms reach the torso or move only slightly behind it. Continuing farther usually turns the movement into chest thrusting or forward glide of the humeral head.

  2. Prone T. Reach the arms long and wide while allowing controlled scapular retraction. Keep the shoulders away from the ears and avoid shortening the movement into a hard shoulder-blade pinch.

  3. Face-pull. Pull toward the upper face while the upper arms move outward and backward. Use the full sequence at face-pull.

Common errors are fully retracting before the arms begin moving, throwing the chest forward, pulling so far backward that the humeral heads move forward in their sockets, shrugging at the finish, and turning every reverse fly into a heavy row.

Isometric scapular stabilization

Isometric stabilization means keeping the scapula controlled against the rib cage while another part of the body moves or carries load. The goal is not to freeze the blade rigidly. It is to prevent unwanted winging, collapsing, shrugging, or twisting.

Keep the collarbones broad, the neck long, and the medial border reasonably flush with the ribs. Do not aggressively squeeze the shoulder blades together.

The useful cue is:

Broad, flush, and quiet.

  1. Bear-crawl hold. Push the floor away until the upper back becomes broad. Keep the chest from sinking between the shoulders and maintain the scapulae flush against the ribs while the knees hover.

  2. Farmer or suitcase carry. Keep the loaded scapula broad and controlled rather than hiking it toward the ear or forcibly dragging it downward. During a suitcase carry, keep the torso vertical rather than leaning away from the weight.

  3. Deadlift or Romanian deadlift. Keep the arms long and the scapulae broad. Use enough lat tension to connect the upper arms to the torso, but do not retract the shoulder blades before pulling or shrug them at lockout.

  4. Pallof press. As the hands travel away from the chest, allow controlled scapular protraction while both blades remain flush. Resist torso rotation without pulling the shoulders backward.

  5. Side plank. Push the supporting forearm into the floor so the supporting shoulder does not collapse. Keep the supporting scapula broad and flush while the trunk remains straight; do not hang passively from the shoulder joint.

  6. Squat setup. Create enough upper-back tension to support the bar and torso position. Do not confuse bracing with aggressively depressing both scapulae or forcing the shoulders into painful retraction.

Posterior tilt

Posterior tilt means that the top of the shoulder blade tips backward while the inferior angle remains controlled against the rib cage. Anterior tilt is the opposite: the top tips forward while the lower edge may become more prominent behind you.

Posterior tilt is difficult to isolate consciously, so use the landmarks. Keep the inferior angle attached to the ribs while the upper edge and acromion roll backward. Reach upward or forward without allowing the front of the shoulder to dump forward, and keep the ribs stacked rather than manufacturing range through spinal extension.

The clearest cue is:

Top edge back; bottom point stays attached.

Serratus fatigue has been shown to reduce posterior tilt and increase scapular internal rotation during higher-angle arm elevation, supporting the emphasis on controlled serratus endurance rather than merely producing maximal force.

  1. Wall slide + lift-off. As the arms rise, move the inferior angles outward and upward while pressing lightly into the wall. During the lift-off, keep the inferior angles against the ribs and tip the upper scapulae backward rather than arching the torso away from the wall.

  2. Arnold press. Keep the inferior angle wrapped against the ribs while the upper edge rolls backward near the top. See Arnold press.

  3. Landmine press. As the arm reaches forward and upward, allow the lower scapula to move outward around the ribs while the upper blade tips backward. Do not obtain the finish by flaring the ribs.

  4. Prone or cable Y raise. Reach the fingertips long while the inferior angles rotate outward and the upper scapulae tip backward. Stop before the movement becomes spinal extension or a neck-dominant shrug.

  5. Face-pull to external rotation. Use face-pull.

Common errors are flaring the ribs to imitate overhead range, allowing the front of the shoulder to spill forward, pulling the blades downward instead of allowing upward rotation, and attempting to create posterior tilt by merely squeezing the shoulder blades together.

Protraction

Protraction means that the scapula glides forward and outward around the rib cage, away from the spine. It is the shoulder-blade action used during reaching and punching. It is not the same as rounding the entire upper back.

Reach long through the arm while allowing the scapula to travel around the ribs. Spread the shoulder blades without shrugging and create the final part of the movement from the scapula rather than additional elbow extension or spinal flexion.

The useful cue is:

Wrap the scapula around the ribs.

Push-up-plus and punching or hugging patterns produce substantial serratus activity, particularly when protraction is combined with an upwardly rotated scapular position.

  1. Push-up plus. Keep the elbows straight during the plus portion and push the floor or handles away until the upper back becomes broad. The medial borders should remain controlled while the blades wrap around the ribs. Do not create the motion by rounding the lower back or tucking the head.

  2. Cable punch. Begin with the scapula neutral rather than pinned backward. Reach the hand forward while moving the entire blade around the rib cage, keeping the medial border flush and the torso still. The final inch should come from scapular protraction.

  3. Dynamic hug or cable press-around. Begin with the arms open and the scapulae allowed to retract modestly. As the arms arc forward, let the blades protract and wrap around the ribs. Finish by reaching around the torso rather than simply bringing the hands together or rounding the spine.

  4. Bear-crawl hold. Use bear-crawl hold.

  5. Landmine press. Use landmine press.

Flat bench and close-grip bench are not meaningful free-protraction exercises because the bench restricts scapular travel.

Retraction

Retraction means that the scapula glides toward the spine. It occurs during rows, face-pulls, reverse flies, and retraction shrugs. Retraction should usually be followed by controlled protraction as the weight returns.

Let the scapula glide forward during the eccentric, then allow the elbow and blade to move backward together during the pull. Do not throw the chest forward or pull the blade maximally downward.

The useful cue is:

Let the blade glide back; do not clamp it down.

  1. Rows. At the stretched position, allow the scapula to glide forward without losing trunk position. During the pull, move the elbow and scapula backward together. Stop before the humeral head rolls forward or the torso twists, then deliberately let the blade move forward again during the return.

  2. Kelso shrug. Remain chest-supported with the arms straight or nearly straight. Retract the scapulae without bending the elbows, lifting the chest from the pad, or dragging the shoulders downward. Let the blades protract again under control between repetitions.

  3. Reverse pec deck or rear-delt fly. Use reverse fly.

  4. Face-pull to external rotation. Use face-pull.

  5. Prone T or W. Use prone T or prone W.

Common errors are holding the blades permanently retracted, pulling the scapula completely backward before the arm moves, twisting the torso to create additional range, and mistaking hard depression for retraction.

Scapular depression and downward rotation

Depression means that the scapula moves downward on the rib cage. Downward rotation means that the inferior angle moves back toward the spine as the arm lowers. These actions occur during pulldowns, straight-arm pulldowns, carries, and the return from overhead positions. They should not become a permanent “shoulders down” posture.

Allow the scapula to rise and upwardly rotate at the top of a pulldown, then let it depress and downwardly rotate as the arm descends. Keep the neck long without violently dragging the shoulders toward the back pockets.

  1. Neutral-grip pulldown or pull-up. Begin from a controlled long reach rather than a pre-depressed shoulder position. As the elbows descend, let the blades downwardly rotate and depress naturally. Do not initiate by jerking the shoulders downward while the elbows remain straight.

  2. One-arm kneeling cable pulldown. At the top, let the scapula rotate upward and travel slightly around the rib cage. Pull the elbow toward the side of the torso while the blade downwardly rotates. Keep the torso from leaning or twisting to manufacture range.

  3. Straight-arm pulldown. Begin with the scapulae allowed to upwardly rotate. As the arms sweep toward the thighs, let the inferior angles travel downward and inward while the blades remain flush. Do not create range through lumbar extension.

  4. High cable lat row to hip. Reach forward and upward enough for the scapula to protract and upwardly rotate. Then pull the elbow toward the hip while the scapula moves down and back without twisting the torso.

  5. Carries and hinges. Use carry and hinge.

Scapular fixation during bench-supported pressing

Fixation means that the scapula remains relatively stable against a surface while the upper arm moves. During bench pressing, the bench restricts normal scapular travel, making the movement fundamentally different from a push-up, cable punch, or unsupported overhead press.

Establish stable upper-back contact with mild retraction rather than an extreme shoulder-blade pinch. Keep the fronts of the shoulders from rolling forward at the bottom, but do not force the blades aggressively downward.

The useful cue is:

Stable against the bench, not crushed into it.

  1. Flat bench or close-grip paused bench. Maintain stable scapular contact throughout the repetition. Do not attempt a dramatic push-up-plus motion at lockout because the bench physically prevents normal protraction. At the bottom, keep the shoulder centered rather than allowing the humeral head to roll forward.

  2. Cable fly or pec deck. When the torso is supported by a pad, use modest stability rather than trying to force a large reach. If the setup allows free scapular movement, permit controlled protraction as the arms come forward.

The main error is applying the push-up-plus cue to a movement in which the bench physically prevents normal scapular motion.

Scaption

Scaption means raising the arm in the scapular plane, slightly forward of directly out to the side. It is approximately midway between a strict front raise and a strict lateral raise.

Raise the arm in the direction the shoulder blade faces, usually around 20–30 degrees forward of the torso. Allow the scapula to upwardly rotate and avoid pulling the shoulder downward as the arm rises. A neutral grip or slight thumbs-up position is generally easier to control than internal rotation.

  1. Cable or dumbbell lateral raise. Raise the arms slightly forward of directly sideways. Let the inferior angles rotate outward and upward while the medial borders remain flush. A small amount of natural elevation is appropriate; a pure shrug is not.

  2. Cable scaption or Y raise. Raise the arms on a forward diagonal rather than directly sideways. Reach outward and upward while the inferior angles rotate around the ribs. Stop before the ribs flare or the neck becomes the primary mover.

  3. Arnold press. Use Arnold press.

  4. Wall slide. Use wall slide.

  5. Landmine press. Use landmine press.

Wall slides can elicit meaningful serratus activity at and above 90 degrees of arm elevation, making them useful practice for overhead scapular control rather than only low-angle protraction.

Common errors are raising the arms directly sideways while internally rotating them, forcing the shoulders down throughout elevation, and arching the torso to create additional range.

Upward rotation

Upward rotation means that the inferior angle moves outward and upward around the ribs, turning the shoulder socket upward as the arm rises. Serratus anterior works with the trapezius to produce this motion. Upper-trapezius activity is not inherently wrong; the problem is a pure shoulder hike without sufficient movement of the inferior angle.

Do not merely think “lift the shoulder.” Track the inferior angle. It should travel away from the spine, around the side of the rib cage, and upward toward the armpit. The medial border should remain reasonably flush, the socket should turn upward, and the upper scapula should posteriorly tilt near the top.

The useful general cue is:

Move the bottom point around and up, not merely the whole shoulder toward the ear.

Arnold press

During the lower portion, keep the scapula broad and flat against the ribs rather than deliberately retracting it.

As the arm begins to rise, move the inferior angle away from the spine and around the rib cage. Through the middle of the range, continue sweeping that bottom point outward and upward toward the armpit. This is the portion most likely to be missed when the arm moves but the scapula remains behind.

Scapular external rotation occurs at the same time. The socket side of the blade wraps forward around the ribs while the medial border becomes flatter against the thorax. This is separate from rotating the dumbbell or upper-arm bone.

Near the top, keep the inferior angle wrapped outward and attached to the ribs while the upper edge and acromion roll backward into posterior tilt. Allow the entire scapula to rise slightly, but only alongside continued outward rotation of the inferior angle. If the shoulder moves toward the ear while the inferior angle remains near the spine, that is predominantly a shrug rather than useful upward rotation.

During the descent, reverse the same path gradually. The inferior angle travels downward and then inward while the medial border remains controlled against the rib cage. Do not abruptly clamp the scapula backward as soon as the dumbbell passes eye level.

The complete cue is:

Bottom point around and up; inner edge flat; top edge rolls back.

Other upward-rotation movements

  1. Wall slide + lift-off. Use wall slide.

  2. Landmine press. Use landmine press.

  3. Lateral raise. Use lateral raise.

  4. Prone or cable Y raise. Use prone Y or scaption.

  5. Pulldown top position. At the top, permit upward rotation before reversing into the pull; do not keep the blades permanently depressed.

Common errors are keeping the blades “back and down,” creating only a neck-dominant shrug, allowing the medial border to wing, and using rib flare or spinal extension instead of scapular rotation.

Program rationale and audit

Why the program is structured this way

Your priorities are deliberately unequal:

  • High priority: scapular control, back development, arms, and the QL/trunk weak spot.
  • Moderate priority: legs, with conservative knee loading and more substantial posterior-chain work.
  • Specific strength goal: progress the bench from approximately 195 pounds toward 225.
  • Lower priority: additional chest hypertrophy, because your chest is already comparatively developed.
  • Separate conditioning: treadmill or 4×4 work performed as desired, but still counted mentally as lower-body loading.

The seven sessions therefore have seven mechanically different jobs:

  1. Knee-light lower body and QL.
  2. Back width and vertical pulling.
  3. Bench strength, chest, and triceps.
  4. Back thickness and horizontal pulling.
  5. Delts and loaded upward rotation.
  6. Posterior chain and QL.
  7. Direct arm specialization.

The scapular rehabilitation is not treated as an identical circuit pasted onto every workout. Each upper session teaches the scapula to do the job that belongs to that session:

  • Vertical pulling teaches an upwardly rotated reach at the top followed by controlled depression and downward rotation.
  • Bench pressing teaches stable scapular fixation, bracketed by free protraction before and after pressing.
  • Horizontal pulling teaches controlled protraction into the stretch, retraction during the pull, and horizontal abduction.
  • Shoulder pressing teaches upward rotation, external rotation, posterior tilt, and controlled elevation.
  • Arm training includes low-fatigue serratus and cuff work without becoming another chest, back, or shoulder workout.

Conscious scapular control can measurably alter scapular orientation and the activation balance among the trapezius and serratus muscles. However, focused scapular retraining has not consistently outperformed well-designed general exercise for clinical outcomes, and the evidence for changing pain or long-term kinematics remains mixed. The correct interpretation is therefore that cues and dedicated drills are useful motor practice layered into progressive lifting, not that every repetition must achieve one imagined perfect scapular pose.

Normal arm elevation involves scapular motion from the beginning, principally increasing upward rotation and posterior tilt, with a trend toward external rotation. That is why “pin the shoulder blades back and down” is not used as a universal shoulder cue.

The exercise variety is systematic rather than random. Distinct exercises are used where they provide meaningfully different mechanics; redundant substitutions are avoided. Research on exercise variation similarly suggests that purposeful variation may help, whereas excessive or biomechanically redundant variation can make progression worse rather than better.

Priority exercises are placed early because the movements performed first tend to receive the greatest strength improvement. Low-fatigue rehabilitation may precede them when it improves positioning without creating meaningful fatigue; otherwise, rehabilitation is placed later.

One focused bench session can still drive the 225-pound goal. Training frequency itself has little independent effect on hypertrophy when total volume is equated, while sufficient volume and progressive loading matter more. Heavier loading is particularly useful for improving one-repetition-maximum strength, which is why the bench receives a heavy top set and substantial back-off work even though chest is not emphasized elsewhere.

What a well-rounded program should cover

A comprehensive bodybuilding and strength program should address horizontal and vertical pushing and pulling; elbow flexion and extension; a knee-dominant pattern; a hip hinge; hip extension and knee flexion; calves, tibialis, hip abductors, and adductors; trunk control in anti-extension, anti-rotation, anti-lateral flexion, and controlled spinal extension; and the major scapular functions of protraction, retraction, upward and downward rotation, posterior tilt, external rotation, and stabilization.
